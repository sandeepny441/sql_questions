Gradient Descent Storyboard

How a model falls into a local minimum

Gradient descent does not scan the whole loss landscape. It only feels the slope under its feet, steps downhill, recomputes the slope, and repeats. That local, step-by-step view is exactly why it can settle into a nearby valley without ever discovering the deepest valley elsewhere.

Start Somewhere Random

1

Training begins with an arbitrary set of weights. The model lands on one side of the landscape, with no clue yet whether a deeper valley exists farther away.

Read the Local Slope

2

The gradient points uphill. To lower loss, gradient descent moves in the opposite direction, using only local slope information around the current point.

Take the First Downhill Step

3

One update moves the parameters toward a lower-loss spot. The point is better than before, but the algorithm still has not explored the entire landscape.

Recompute and Repeat

4

After every move, the slope changes. Gradient descent checks again and again, taking a chain of locally sensible steps that bend toward the nearest valley floor.

Slow Down Near the Bottom

5

Near the valley floor, the slope gets smaller. Updates shrink and the path may wobble a little from side to side before settling.

Stop at a Local Minimum

6

Once the nearby slope is close to zero, the algorithm has no local reason to leave. It has reached a local minimum, even though a deeper valley may exist somewhere else on the surface.

Core Intuition

Gradient descent is locally smart, not globally all-seeing.

That is the central idea. The algorithm is excellent at following a nearby downhill direction, but it does not know whether another valley behind a hill is lower unless some strategy such as restarts, momentum, noise, or a different initialization helps it discover a new region.

Why Global Minima Are Tricky

The deepest valley may not be the one you should chase blindly.

In principle, a global minimum is the lowest point on the whole loss surface. In practice, gradient-based training usually has no map of the full terrain, may need to move uphill to escape a basin, and can spend huge effort proving global optimality. In deep learning, the deepest training valley is also not guaranteed to be the best at generalizing to new data.

No full landscape view The optimizer only sees local slope where it currently stands, not every valley at once.
Escape may require an uphill move To reach a deeper basin, the path may first need to climb out of the current one, which pure gradient descent resists.
Deepest training point is not always best A sharper global minimum can fit the training set extremely well yet generalize worse than a broader, flatter basin.

A useful mental model: local minima are what gradient descent finds by following nearby slope; global minima are a whole-landscape question.
